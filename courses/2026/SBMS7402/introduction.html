Landscape & Taxonomy of Large-Scale Health Data

Source of the large-scale data

  • Electronic Health Record (EHR)

Medical Claim

Health Insurance Claims Data

The Claims Lifecycle

  1. Patient Encounter: Patient receives care (inpatient admission, outpatient clinic visit, or pharmacy fill).
  2. Medical Coding: Medical coders translate clinical documentation into standardized billing codes (ICD for diagnoses, CPT/HCPCS for procedures, NDC for prescription drugs).
  3. Claim Submission: The provider submits a claim form
  4. Adjudication: The payer evaluates the claim against the patient’s coverage policy, determines negotiated rates, calculates patient cost-sharing and issues payment.

Health Economics

Health Economics and Cost-Effectiveness

Claims represent actual economic transactions:

  • Billed Amount (chargemaster rate - often inflated)
  • Allowed Amount (contractually negotiated rate)
  • Paid Amount (actual money reimbursed by insurer)
  • Out-of-Pocket Cost (patient copay, deductible, coinsurance)

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Billed Amount} = & \underbrace{\text{Paid Amount} + \text{Out-of-Pocket Cost}}_{\text{Allowed Amount}} + \\ & \text{Contractual Adjustment} \end{aligned} \]

Limitation of medical claim

  • Upcoding
    • Assign expensive diagnostic or complex procedure codes to qualify for larger reimbursements
  • Long-term longitudinal follow-up
    • Commerical plan are usually valid for 2-3 years
    • Long-term longitudinal follow-up (e.g., 10-year chronic diseases) is severely censored.
  • Lack of clinical nuances
    • know what have been done
    • Don’t know what are the results

Electronic Health Record

What is an EHR?

An Electronic Health Record (EHR) is the digital chart of a patient’s encounters with the health system—visits, diagnoses, labs, prescriptions, and clinical notes stored so clinicians (and later analysts) can reuse them.

Toy timeline (one patient):

  1. Visit the clinic → demographics recorded
  2. Doctor records a diagnosis → coded (e.g. ICD)
  3. Lab is ordered → result coded (e.g. LOINC)
  4. Prescription is filled → drug coded (e.g. ATC / RxNorm)

Coding systems are a shared language so different hospitals and software can mean the same clinical idea.

Components in EHR System

  • Demographics & Social Determinants: Age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic indicators.
  • Clinical Encounters & Diagnoses: Coded using international diagnostic standards:
    • ICD-9 / ICD-10 / ICD-11: International Classification of Diseases.
    • SNOMED-CT: Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine for clinical concepts.
  • Prescriptions & Pharmacy Records: Coded using ATC or RxNorm frameworks.
  • Laboratory & Diagnostic Results: Coded using LOINC standards.
  • Unstructured Clinical Text: Progress notes, discharge summaries, radiological reports.

From Individual Care to Population & Global Health

  • Scaling clinical observations from single patient encounters to entire populations.

  • Clinical Medicine: Focuses on diagnosis, treatment, and immediate patient outcomes.

  • Population Health: Health outcomes, determinants, and patterns across defined groups (e.g., regional populations, disease cohorts).

  • Global Health & Digital Health Venture: Scalable health solutions, cross-border disease surveillance, and commercialization of data-driven digital health products.

Consider a influenza outbreak in district \(D_1\). and \(D_2\). with two hospitals \(H_A\) (in \(D_1\)). and \(H_B\) (in \(D_2\)). Scientists would like to investigate the incidence rate of the influenza. The fact sheet is below

District Population Active pateients New influenza cases \(H_A\) share \(H_B\) share
\(D_1\) 100K 25K 500 80% 20%
\(D_2\) 200K 50K 1000 30% 70%

\[ \text{Incidence rate} = \frac{\text{New cases in a time interval}}{\text{Total poppulation at risk}} \]

  • True incidence rate \(0.5\%\)

Census data as denominator

Using government census data as denominator:

District Population Case reported in the hospital Rate
\(D_1\) 100,000 \(400\) \(0.4\%\)
\(D_2\) 200,000 \(700\) \(0.35\%\)

If we consider all cases in \(H_A\) with population from \(D_1 + D_2\) the rate will be \(0.13\%\)

  • Cross-Boundary Leakage
    • Residents of District \(D\) do not exclusively visit Hospital in \(D\)
    • The reported rate is underreported
  • Tertiary Referral Inflow
    • Patients travel from other regions or abroad for treatment.
    • The reported rate is overreported

\(D_1\) 25,000 \(700\) \(2.8\%\)

  • Health-Seeking bias (Berkson’s Bias)
    • Healthy individuals with zero medical encounters/ new immigrants will be excluded

  • The “Ghost Patient” & Churn Problem
    • Patients could migrate, die, or change health systems.
    • The reported rate is underreported

or

  1. Correct on the denominater: $P_{D_1} = 0.8 n_{D_1} + 0.3 n_{D_2} $

Think twice about the context

  • Healthcare system Close v.s Open
    • Closed / Single-Payer Networks (e.g., Hong Kong Hospital Authority, UK NHS, Veterans Affairs): High population coverage (up to 90%+ of inpatient care) minimizes leakage,
    • geographic census denominators much more viable.
  • Open / Multi-Payer Systems (e.g., US EHRs):
    • Prone to denominator fragmentation.
    • Insurance claims enrollment periods rather than pure EHR visits to establish continuous coverage.
  • Disease context
    • Serious/ rare disease are more often in the medical centers

Hong Kong Hospital Authority is more or less in between Single-Payer Networks and Open systems. (Depends on the diseases)

EHR as an evidence

Problem Traditional Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) are

  • expensive,
  • slow, and often lack generalizability.

EHR can

Regulatory bodies (e.g., US FDA, EMA, NMPA) increasingly accept real-world evidence (RWE)—evidence generated from routine care data such as EHRs, not only from traditional trials—for

  • Post-market surveillance,
  • Label expansions, and
  • Synthetic control arms in clinical trials.

Challenges in EHR

Missingness (missing for a reason)

  • Clinic vignette: sicker patients get more labs ordered; healthier patients may have blank lab fields. A “missing” lab is often informative, not random noise.
  • Formal label: this pattern is often called missing not at random (MNAR) (contrast: completely random gaps vs gaps explained by other recorded variables).
  • Labs/tests are ordered based on clinical suspicion, leading to missing baseline data.
  • Loss to follow-up due to stigma and/or economic difficulties.

Challenges in EHR

Selection / Sampling Bias

  • EHRs represent health-seeking populations, not necessarily the general population.
  • People who are not covered in the health care system

Measurement / Coding Error

  • Inaccurate ICD codes or changes in institutional coding policies over time.
  • Human errors in the process
  • Misdiagnosis
