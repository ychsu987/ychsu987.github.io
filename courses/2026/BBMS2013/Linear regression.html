Overview of linear regression

In linear regression, our fundamental objective is to explain the variability observed in the outcome variable (\(Y\)) using one or more explanatory variable (\(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k\)).

\[ \hat{Y}_i = b_0 + b_1 X_{1i} + b_2 X_{2i} + \dots + b_k X_{ki} \]

It is crucial to know

  • Does \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_k\) really make explaination to \(Y\)
  • How good is the model performance?

Evaluating linear regression - from an observation

  • The regression diagnostics lies in the Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)
  • We can decompose the error of you prediction as:

\[ (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i) = (Y_i - \bar{Y})-(\hat{Y}_i - \bar{Y}) \]

Where:

  • \((Y_i - \hat{Y}_i) = e_i\) = Unexplained residual deviation (the error remaining after fitting the model).
  • \((Y_i - \bar{Y})\) = Total deviation of the data point from the baseline benchmark (sample mean).
  • \((\hat{Y}_i - \bar{Y})\) = Explained deviation (the difference between the model’s fitted value and the sample mean).

A summary of error

Squaring both sides and summing across all \(n\) observations yields the fundamental sum-of-squares identity:

\[ \text{SSE} = \text{SST} - \text{SSR} \]

Where

  • SST (Total Sum of Squares): \(\sum_i (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2\)
  • SSE (Error Sum of Squares): \(\sum_i (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2\)
  • SSR (Regression Sum of Squares): \(\sum_i (\hat{Y}_i - \bar{Y})^2\)

(Note on terminology: In some literature, notably British and econometrics, SSR is termed “Explained Sum of Squares” (ESS) and SSE is termed “Residual Sum of Squares” (RSS). Always check definitions in your specific context).

Mathematical Formulations & Intuitions

Metric Formula Degrees of Freedom (\(df\)) Conceptual Intuition
SST \(\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2\) \(n - 1\) Total baseline variation in the outcome \(Y\) without using any predictors.
SSR \(\sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{Y}_i - \bar{Y})^2\) \(k\) Variation successfully accounted for by the regression model.
SSE \(\sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)^2\)
\(= \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2\)		 \(n - k - 1\) Unexplained variation; sum of squared discrepancies between observed points and regression surface.
  • \(n\) represents the sample size
  • \(k\) represents the number of predictors, excluding the intercept.

Goodness-of-Fit: \(R^2\)

\(R^2\) (The coefficient of determination) quantifies the proportion of total variation in the response variable explained by the linear combination of predictors:

\[ R^2 = \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} \]

Interpretation Rules

  • Range: In standard OLS with an intercept, \(0 \le R^2 \le 1\) (or \(0\%\) to \(100\%\)).
  • Interpretation: If \(R^2 = 0.74\), exactly 74% of the variability in \(Y\) is explained by the predictors included in the model, while the remaining 26% is attributable to residual noise or omitted factors.
  • Limitation: \(R^2\) is non-decreasing with respect to the addition of predictors. Adding completely unrelated random noise variables will force \(\text{SSE}\) to stay equal or decrease, artificially inflating \(R^2\).

Adjusted \(R^2\) (\(R^2_{\text{adj}}\))

The Adjusted \(R^2\) incorporates degrees of freedom to penalize excessive parameterization and prevent overfitting, :

\[ R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \left[ \frac{\text{SSE} / (n - k - 1)}{\text{SST} / (n - 1)} \right] = 1 - \left( \frac{\text{MSE}}{\text{MST}} \right) \]

Alternatively expressed via \(R^2\): \[ R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2) \frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1} \]

Key Properties

  • Increases only if a newly added predictor reduces \(\text{SSE}\) by a margin greater than what would be expected by pure chance.
  • Can occasionally yield a negative value when model fit is negligible; in such cases, it is effectively interpreted as zero.

\(F\)-Test for Model Significance

\(F\)-Test** evaluates whether the collection of predictors collectively explains a statistically significant amount of variation.

Hypotheses

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): All slope coefficients are simultaneously zero.

\[ H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_k = 0 \]

  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\)): At least one slope coefficient is non-zero.

\[ H_1: \text{There is at least one} \beta_j \neq 0 \]

Difference on individual testing on \(\beta_i\)

  • \(\beta_i\) is a \(t\)-test
  • Repeated testing \(\beta_i\) cause p-value inflation

Mean Squares: Variance Between vs. Variance Within

Before calculating the \(F\)-statistic, we normalize the sums of squares by their respective degrees of freedom:

Mean Square Regression (MSR):

\[ \text{MSR} = \frac{\text{SSR}}{k} \]

Mean Square Error (MSE):

\[ \text{MSE} = \frac{\text{SSE}}{n - k - 1} = s_e^2 \quad (\text{an unbiased estimator of } \sigma^2) \]

Constructing the \(F\)-Statistic

The \(F\)-statistic is the ratio of explained variance per degree of freedom to unexplained variance per degree of freedom:

\[ F = \frac{\text{MSR}}{\text{MSE}} = \frac{\text{SSR} / k}{\text{SSE} / (n - k - 1)} \]

It can also be written directly in terms of \(R^2\):

\[ F = \frac{R^2 / k}{(1 - R^2) / (n - k - 1)} \]

Sampling Distribution & Decision Rule:

Under the null hypothesis \(H_0\), and assuming normally distributed homoscedastic errors: \[ F \sim F(df_1 = k, \, df_2 = n - k - 1) \]

  • Decision: Reject \(H_0\) at significance level \(\alpha\) if \(F > F_{\text{crit}}(\alpha, k, n - k - 1)\) or if \(p\text{-value} < \alpha\).
  • Conclusion: Rejecting \(H_0\) confirms that the regression model explains significantly more variance than a naive “intercept-only” model (sample mean \(\bar{Y}\)).

Simple Linear Regression: \(t\)-test vs. \(F\)-test

In simple linear regression (\(k = 1\)): \[ F_{(1, n-2)} = (t_{\beta_1})^2 \]

The two-sided \(t\)-test on the single slope coefficient \(\beta_1\) and the overall \(F\)-test are mathematically identical and yield the exact same \(p\)-value.

High \(F\)-statistic, but no individual significant \(t\)-statistics?

This classic paradox typically occurs due to severe multicollinearity.

  • The predictors are highly correlated with each other.
  • The overall \(F\)-test confirms that the set of predictors collectively accounts for substantial variation.
  • However, the variance inflation factor (VIF) is high, inflating individual standard errors and making it impossible for the individual \(t\)-tests to isolate which specific variable is doing the work.

Low \(R^2\) with a Statistically Significant \(F\)-test

  • Common in noisy biological or social science data (e.g., \(R^2 = 0.08\), \(p < 0.001\) with \(n = 5,000\)).
  • This means the effect is genuine, real, and reproducible (not a sampling artifact), but individual-level variation remains high due to unmeasured factors.

Pitfalls to Avoid

  1. Confusing \(R^2_{\text{adj}}\) with Causality: A high \(R^2\) indicates strong linear correlation, not causal direction.
  2. Comparing \(R^2_{\text{adj}}\) Across Different Transformations: You cannot compare the \(R^2\) of a model predicting \(Y\) directly to a model predicting \(\ln(Y)\), because \(\text{SST}\) is measured on fundamentally different scales.
  3. Over-reliance on \(R^2_{\text{adj}}\) Alone: Always inspect residual diagnostics (residual vs. fitted plots, Q-Q plots) to ensure linearity, normality, and homoscedasticity assumptions hold.
